Overview

Thief arrived in the crowded 1981 maze-chase market with a getaway-car theme, varied street layouts and recorded police-radio dialogue. Its basic relationship to Pac-Man is apparent, while the pursuit setting, criminal ranks and sound give Pacific Novelty’s game a distinct presentation.

The player drives a getaway car through city streets while police cars converge. Collecting money provides the main objective, and dollar-sign power items allow the car to run police off the road. A criminal-rank ladder, progressing from petty offenses to Public Enemy #1, turns repeated play into an exaggerated criminal career. These elements give the maze-chase rules a consistent setting and a sense of escalation.

The original upright cabinet

Pacific Novelty did not sell Thief as a bare imitation of an established maze game. Its surviving 1981 two-sided flyer turns the machine into a little crime-film attraction: the screen images are presented as a first episode, a second episode and a finale, while the physical upright sits alongside them as the main attraction. This was an operator sales document, not merely an advertisement aimed at players. It combined the promise of varied play with the practical selling points of the machine itself.

The factory flyer identifies an upright approximately 24½ inches wide, 69 inches tall and 30 inches deep. It promotes a Formica-finished cabinet and “quadraphonic” sound. The illustrated machine has a notably wide upper speaker panel, a colorful crime-themed marquee and a compact control area above the coin door. These are specifications and images from Pacific Novelty’s own marketing; they should not be mistaken for measurements of every surviving example or a photograph of a machine as restored today.

The sales sheet also declares the game already in release and available to locations, although it gives no month of first shipment. Its theatrical “Coming Attractions” headline was part of the movie theme, not independent evidence that operators still had to wait for the game. The source helps document the cabinet Pacific Novelty intended operators to recognize—and explains why a correct marquee, speaker panel and cabinet finish matter to restorers. Source: Pacific Novelty, original 1981 two-sided Thief flyer, preserved by the International Arcade Museum.

How it plays

At the most basic level, Thief asks the player to drive through a maze, collect money and evade police. Ordinary greenbacks are worth 10 points each. Police cars do not just lumber randomly; they converge, cut off routes and force quick directional judgments. Side tunnels can abruptly change an escape line. The dollar-sign power items let the player smash pursuing police cars for escalating point values—100, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 if chained effectively.

Thief uses eight distinct maze layouts, ranging from open escape routes to cramped streets with tight corners and dangerous intersections. After all eight, the sequence repeats at greater speed and with a shorter power phase. Players must learn several street plans while adapting to faster pursuit and reduced opportunities to attack the police. That structure makes the game read almost like a sequence of urban set pieces rather than a single abstract board. A long game is not simply a matter of surviving one repeated pattern forever; it involves memorizing several layouts and then handling them again under greater pressure.

The game’s eight different maps should not be confused with its sixteen-stage progression. Pacific Novelty’s flyer promised an opening labyrinth and fifteen more; the playable game uses eight unique street layouts, then revisits those layouts in faster, more hazardous versions for stages nine through sixteen. After the sixteenth, the higher-difficulty cycle begins again rather than introducing eight entirely new maps. The distinction reconciles the manufacturer’s original “sixteen” sales claim with the fewer unique maze designs visible in play. The criminal-title ladder advances with cleared stages, so changing titles and increasing pressure provide another sense of progression even when a familiar street plan returns. Source: MobyGames, arcade gameplay documentation; 1981 manufacturer flyer.

Operator settings, controls and service

The surviving owner’s manual documents Thief as a working coin-operated machine rather than an unchangeable set of gameplay rules. Operators could use DIP switches to set the price of a game, the number of cars available at the start, whether bonus cars were awarded, and whether extra cars arrived only once or repeatedly at selected score intervals. Pacific Novelty recommended enabling bonus cars and allowing repeat awards; its suggested interval was 10,000 points, with higher thresholds also available. These options mattered because a difficult maze game’s average play duration—and the number of quarters a location could expect—depended in part on how generously the machine was set.

Movement was handled through a four-direction joystick, with separate one-player and two-player start switches. The cabinet’s documentation also includes an upright/cocktail setting, a detail worth distinguishing from the later licensed GDI table cabinet. The operator was expected to check and adjust ordinary physical controls, not just change settings on a screen. The manual identifies WICO-replaceable front-panel parts and describes care for the joystick contacts and coin mechanism. These period instructions establish the actual operator-facing controls; unverified later summaries sometimes invent extra player action buttons.

The manufacturer included unusually thorough fault-finding routines. Its tests covered ROM checksums and memory, switches on the control panel and coin door, color bars and crosshatch patterns for the video system, and output drivers for sound and mechanical functions. The coin door’s normally-open slam switch could also signal a change of diagnostic test. A service technician could therefore separate a defective input, display problem or sound-system failure from a problem in the game program. The manual even discusses using a degaussing coil when transporting a color monitor leaves magnetized, blotchy patches. Source: Pacific Novelty, Thief owner’s manual, operations and diagnostics sections (archived transcription).

The criminal-rank ladder

One of Thief’s cleverest touches is the mock criminal-career ladder. As the player progresses, the game assigns and upgrades titles ranging from petty misdemeanor humor up through more grandiose outlaw status. Surviving sources preserve ranks such as Loitering, Joyriding, Petty Thief, Car Theft, Grand Theft Auto, Professional Thief, Jewel Thief, The Boss and ultimately Public Enemy #1. The ranks add continuity to successive stages and reinforce the cabinet’s crime-film theme. They also make the player’s progress legible to spectators, who can follow both the chase and the increasingly grandiose criminal status it confers.

Audio and presentation

The cabinet’s cassette mechanism plays an extended loop of police pursuit dialogue. References to streets, locations and progress reports, often with a Los Angeles flavor, connect the moving cars to an ongoing police response. The recordings provide a sustained setting for the action beyond the electronic sounds generated by the game.

That soundtrack depended on a very physical piece of equipment. The original manual calls for a leaderless, auto-reversing cassette in a car-stereo-style player. It tells technicians to maintain the mechanism and, if the special game tape stops playing, to try an ordinary cassette first: a deck that plays a normal tape but not the game cassette suggests worn or damaged tape media. The cabinet was therefore vulnerable to failures that a solid-state sound board alone would not suffer—mechanical wear, tape trouble and missing or disconnected audio hardware.

The recorded dialogue and the electronic effects were not identical parts of the system. The game’s service tests describe a programmable sound generator as well as switched outputs for tape power, a talking track and an explosion/crash track. Modern MAME hardware documentation identifies two General Instrument AY-3-8910 programmable sound generators, while the cassette recording supplies the extended speech material. The original flyer advertised quadraphonic sound, and a collector documenting a 2013 reconstruction described the Pacific Novelty cabinet’s four-speaker panel. Those facts support a multi-speaker original presentation, but they should not be inflated into an independently verified claim about four discrete recorded tape channels. Original owner’s manual; MAME technical listing; collector’s firsthand build record.

Period reception

Electronic Games treated the audio as the feature that elevated Thief above a crowded maze-chase field. The magazine later revisited the title in awards coverage, giving it a Certificate of Merit and again emphasizing the sound.

This feature was strong enough that it confused contemporary coverage. Electronic Games initially described the voices as coming from an advanced speech-chip solution. Pacific Novelty and industry technicians later corrected the magazine, which printed an explicit correction explaining that the sound was actually provided by a tape-loop system. The correction demonstrates how distinctive the cabinet seemed even to informed contemporary observers.

Recorded audio gives Thief its sustained police-pursuit atmosphere.

The cassette system is also important to preservation. A surviving machine with a missing or inoperative audio mechanism retains its game program but loses much of the sound that shaped the original experience.

Movie-style credits

Thief’s in-program credits imitate a film: Philip Lieberman is writer and screenplay author, Brian D. Semler executive producer, Bernie Stolar director, Bill Cravens the provider of “stunts,” and Robert Meagher responsible for cinematography. Their playful presentation preserves named contributions that are rarely so well documented for a small early-1980s arcade company.

The film-credit format complements the company’s use of recorded voices in Shark Attack and police-radio chatter in Thief. In each case, presentation gives the machine a theatrical character beyond its immediate rules.

The licensed GDI cocktail version, 1982

The original Pacific Novelty upright was not the only commercial form of Thief. A previously overlooked April 15, 1982 Play Meter advertisement explicitly identifies a table-format Thief as manufactured by GDI Inc. under license from Pacific Novelty of Marina del Rey, California. That matters: GDI was not the game's original creator, nor was its machine simply a dealer reselling the upright. The advertisement documents an authorized manufacturing arrangement for a different cabinet format. Source: Play Meter, April 15, 1982, p. 57, GDI advertisement.

Additional contemporary reporting confirms that the table model moved beyond a drawing. In its April 24, 1982 coin-machine coverage, Cash Box quoted GDI marketing director Bob Breither announcing the new cocktail game. Breither said samples were being sent to the company’s distributors, with larger shipments to follow. He described favorable test earnings in arcades, game rooms and street locations, but those were the manufacturer’s promotional claims; the report supplied no independent collection figures or total number of machines delivered. The same account described the familiar gameplay: blue police cars chasing a getaway car through dollar-lined streets, with temporary opportunities to smash the pursuers after collecting a dollar sign. Source: Cash Box, April 24, 1982, p. 39.

The GDI version appears again in contemporary equipment directories. A November 29, 1982 Game Machine listing places “The Thief (table)” under GDI with an April 1982 date. An additional GDI-specific Thief owner’s manual survives in arcade-manual indexes, alongside Pacific Novelty’s original manual. Together, the advertisement, news report and equipment listing establish a documented 1982 licensed cocktail release, although the precise production quantity and any hardware differences between versions still require direct inspection of the GDI manual and a verified surviving cabinet. Source: Game Machine, November 29, 1982, equipment directory; ArcadeRTFM manual index.

Commercial position

Thief is Pacific Novelty’s best-known surviving game. More examples are documented than for the company’s other dedicated machines, and later score records attest to continued play. Neither surviving cabinets nor score reports, however, supplies a reliable 1981 factory production total.

Thief was already attracting attention at the 1981 AMOA trade show. In Play Meter’s January 1, 1982 roundup of games seen there, independent reviewer Roger C. Sharpe ranked Thief third among his personal five choices, behind Galaga and Tempest. The game did not make the magazine’s combined panel top five. Source: Play Meter, January 1, 1982, p. 24.

By June 15, 1982, Kansas City distributor Pyramid Distributing advertised Thief among games available for immediate delivery, claiming it was showing encouraging results. That was a dealer’s sales pitch, not an independent earnings report. Source: Play Meter, June 15, 1982, distributor advertisement. The magazine’s November 15, 1982 year-end top-20 chart (p. 65) did not list Thief. Its ratings averaged placement points across operator polls; the absence supplies neither a Thief-specific revenue figure nor proof that the game lost money.

National score reporting recorded Brian Wathen of Owensboro, Kentucky at 6,130,930 points in early 1983, demonstrating committed play after the launch period. Classic Arcade Gaming’s later historical reconstruction records Mike Ziara at 9,456,120 points at the Golden Dome Arcade in Salisbury, Maryland on April 25, 1983. Modern VAPS census participation provides a separate record of surviving cabinets. Together with the score history, it supports Thief’s position as Pacific Novelty’s most visible surviving game, while leaving original production and sales totals unresolved.

Evidence that could clarify sales

A period distributor report, sales ledger, operator earnings chart or serial-number study could clarify how Thief’s later visibility compares with its original commercial performance.

Preserving the original experience

The survival story of Thief is not limited to whole cabinets. Separate marquees, bezels, control-panel overlays, board stacks and harnesses can survive even after their original wooden enclosures have disappeared. In 2013, a collector using the name Phetishboy documented acquiring original Thief parts and fitting them to a modified Stern-style cabinet. His account describes the practical differences that made a credible reconstruction difficult, especially the original Pacific Novelty arrangement of four small speaker openings rather than the single opening in the donor cabinet. The resulting project is valuable evidence about preservation, but it must be labeled a reconstruction, not passed off as a surviving factory-built Thief upright.

That same builder documented getting the tape interface running with an original Pacific Novelty game board stack and trying recordings from other Pacific Novelty machines to establish which sound feed carried voices and which handled crash effects. The account makes the preservation problem tangible: an owner might possess a working program and screen, yet still have to locate or repair the appropriate deck, interface, tape, speaker arrangement and cabinet artwork to reproduce the original experience. Source: firsthand 2013 collector restoration thread, Museum of the Game forums.

Emulation addresses a different part of that problem. Phil Stroffolino’s early MAME work brought Thief into arcade emulation in 2000, with separate support added for the Pacific Novelty cassette recordings that July. Because the speech was originally analog audio rather than bytes generated by a synthesizer, a ROM set alone does not preserve the complete soundtrack. Modern recordings and replacement samples can help make the game playable, but the original tape, sound-routing hardware and speaker arrangement remain distinct physical evidence. Source: MAME development history and machine record.

Why Thief matters

Thief brings together several features of Pacific Novelty’s work: familiar rules adapted through a distinctive theme, recorded sound as a major part of the cabinet, and unusually informative personnel credits. Its eight mazes and criminal-rank progression give players several forms of continuing challenge. Continuing score records and cabinet preservation also provide a more substantial afterlife than is documented for the company’s other videogames. That record makes Thief a useful introduction to both Pacific Novelty and the later history of its machines.

Contribute

Marquee and cabinet photographs, manuals, operator paperwork, score records, serial numbers and corrections could help document Thief more fully. Contributions with clear provenance are particularly useful, and contributors can be credited with permission.
