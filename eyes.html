Eyes (1982): Digitrex, Techstar and Rock-Ola

If one game can be used to explain how Techstar operated, it is Eyes. Released in 1982, the colorful maze game became Techstar's best-documented arcade title and brought the small Miami company into business with two established coin-op manufacturers: Rock-Ola in the United States and Zaccaria in Europe. It also demonstrates why Techstar's history has to be handled carefully. Depending on which surviving cabinet, title screen, advertisement, or database entry is examined, Eyes can appear to be a Rock-Ola game, a Zaccaria game, a Techstar game, or a Digitrex game. Each description contains part of the truth.

Sanchez, Digitrex, And Techstar

The clearest starting point is the original U.S. copyright record. Eyes was registered as an unpublished videogame on March 3, 1982. The record identifies Luis Sanchez, doing business as Digitrex, as the author and identifies Techstar, Inc. as the copyright claimant. That distinction is important. The surviving evidence supports Sanchez and Digitrex as the creative source of the game while Techstar controlled rights to it. It does not justify simply replacing those credits with "developed by Techstar." Digitrex itself was a real Florida corporation, separate from Techstar. Period material occasionally blended the two names commercially, including the wording "Digitrex Techstar" seen within one version of the program, but no surviving corporate record establishes that as the legal name of a combined company. The exact contractual relationship between Digitrex and Techstar remains unknown.

The Copyright Deposit And The Separate Digitrex Corporation

The official copyright dataset contains more than the short author-and-claimant line. The unpublished Eyes registration, PAu000404740, is dated March 3, 1982 and records a deposit consisting of one film reel and a one-page description submitted in lieu of the videogame itself. The author entry reads "Luis Sanchez d.b.a. Digitrex, employer for hire," while Techstar, Inc. is the claimant. The dataset also records Sanchez's birth year as 1948.

Later firsthand recollection complicates any attempt to turn that copyright line into a definitive hands-on programming credit. A former Techstar engineer, whose identity is withheld at his request, did not recognize Luis Sanchez as the programmer he remembered for Eyes; he recalled the programmer instead as a young European man whose name he could no longer remember. Memory decades after the fact cannot overturn the primary copyright record, but the two sources answer different questions. The registration firmly documents Sanchez / Digitrex on the authorship side and Techstar as claimant; the identity of the person who actually wrote the game code remains unresolved.

Digitrex was not merely a trade name floating around Techstar paperwork. Digitrex, Inc., Florida document number F71321, was filed on March 17, 1982, only two weeks after the Eyes registration. Its address was 2760 N. Bay Road, Miami Beach, Florida 33140. Mineya C. Sanchez was the registered agent; Luis Sanchez was listed as president, treasurer and director, and Mineya C. Sanchez as secretary and vice president. Digitrex filed a 1983 annual report and was involuntarily dissolved on November 21, 1984. None of those corporate records merges Digitrex into Techstar.

Rock-Ola Takes Eyes National

The May 22, 1982 Cash Box report and its signing-photo caption identify Bill Olliges as Techstar president and Ed Miller as a Techstar consultant. They describe the Rock-Ola/Techstar Eyes agreement and indicate that sample upright and cocktail machines were expected to begin reaching distributors during May. Rock-Ola Manufacturing Corporation brought an established American coin-op manufacturing and distribution business to the agreement. Rock-Ola did not merely distribute an anonymous Techstar cabinet. Its surviving documentation shows a formal Rock-Ola product. The upright version was sold as Model G-206, while a cocktail-table version was offered as Model GC-206. Rock-Ola produced sales material, cabinet specifications, parts documentation, and a complete service manual for the game. Its flyer described the machine as manufactured under exclusive license from Digitrex/Techstar.

By July 17, Cash Box reported that Ron Moskal, Rock-Ola’s vice president of sales, said Eyes had held “cash earnings within the top five pieces” during eight to 10 weeks of testing at arcades and locations across the United States. The report also said the game was available through Rock-Ola distributors. A July 24 notice added that, after Rock-Ola’s summer shutdown, production had resumed and Eyes was a priority delivery item that had tested “superbly well.” Together, those dated reports establish nationwide testing and distributor availability by mid-July, while stopping short of claiming a precise first release date.

That single sentence neatly captures the division of roles. Sanchez and Digitrex stand behind the game's authorship. Techstar appears as the rights holder and licensing party. Rock-Ola manufactured and sold its own implementation of the game. The distinction becomes even clearer on the machine itself. Surviving U.S. program versions begin with "ROCK-OLA PRESENTS," but the copyright wording underneath varies. One preserved version credits "COPYRIGHT 1982 DIGITREX TECHSTAR," while another simply displays "©1982 TECHSTAR INC." These variations are evidence of different surviving program sets and commercial presentations. They are not evidence that Rock-Ola suddenly became the original developer.

Eight mazes and a distinctive pursuit game

Eyes belongs to the crowded family of early-1980s maze games that followed Pac-Man, but its presentation is difficult to mistake for anyone else's. Rock-Ola advertised eight different scenes, each with its own maze layout and escalating difficulty. The flyer even gave the stages playful names such as Jazz Man, Right On, Hard Rock, Hot Stuff, Cross Over, Hey Man, Sweet Gal, and Cool Down. The maze display, giant eyeball imagery, vivid colors, and ability to attack enemies gave the game a personality considerably stranger than its simple premise suggests. It was exactly the sort of visually peculiar title that could stand out in an arcade already overflowing with maze games.

Rock-Ola leaned heavily into that visual identity. Its orange sales flyer surrounds the gameplay screen with the enormous word EYES, while the cabinet artwork turned staring eyes into the game's dominant graphic motif.

Eyes Goes To Europe

Italian manufacturer Zaccaria produced a licensed European version of Eyes and gave it one of the most elaborate cabinet treatments associated with the game. Its advertising explicitly presented Eyes as a European exclusive. The cabinet used enormous painted eyes across the front artwork, a heavily decorated control panel, and a marquee that transformed the title into a large geometric logo. Zaccaria's flyer also shows that the company was selling the game as a distinctly Zaccaria product rather than simply importing a Rock-Ola cabinet. The program reflects the same regional adaptation. Where the American sets say "ROCK-OLA PRESENTS," the preserved European program says "ZACCARIA PRESENTS." The underlying copyright remains with Techstar.

These regional presentations reflect different responsibilities within the same licensing network. Rock-Ola manufactured and presented Eyes in the United States, and Zaccaria did so in Europe. Techstar held the rights, while Sanchez and Digitrex remained the documented source of the work.

A Small Hardware Mystery

Rock-Ola’s service documentation uses G-6390-A for the game-board assembly and G-6395-A for the complete game PCB in cabinet and replacement-parts contexts. These are different levels of parts identification within the documentation; the manual does not establish separate board generations. A surviving collector-documented Eyes board introduces a separate curiosity. It carries markings including TECHSTAR-III, Miami 1982, and Made in Japan, along with a Rock-Ola identification sticker and a handwritten C6390-A notation. That board is important, but its meaning is not yet known. Techstar later used other numbered hardware markings, including TECH-II, but there is currently no evidence proving a neat TECH-I, TECH-II, TECHSTAR-III sequence. That hardware puzzle belongs later in the Techstar story.

Survivor Evidence, With Limits

Collector restoration evidence also suggests that at least one Rock-Ola Eyes cabinet may have involved cabinet reuse or conversion, but a single survivor cannot be turned into a factory-wide policy. The broader conclusion is deliberately narrow: individual cabinets can preserve histories of repair, conversion and reuse that do not necessarily describe how every unit left the manufacturer.

The fuller record: how Eyes was made, sold, played and remembered

The short version of Eyes—the title commonly seen in a Rock-Ola cabinet—is only the visible surface. Surviving corporate records, trade reporting, manuals, flyers, program sets and later preservation evidence let the game be placed much more securely in the arcade landscape of 1982.

The corporate trail behind the name Techstar

Official Florida corporation records show that Techstar, Inc. and Digitrex, Inc. were separate Florida corporations.

A necessary distinction: modern shorthand such as “Digitrex Techstar” is useful for identifying the development lineage seen in ROM/preservation records, but it should not be written as though it were one incorporated firm. The evidence instead points to a Luis Sanchez / Digitrex development entity working with Bill Olliges's Techstar, with Techstar holding and licensing the commercial rights to Eyes.

Copyright, authorship and ownership

The indexed U.S. copyright registration for Eyes is PAu000404740, dated March 3, 1982, with a 1982 creation date. The record identifies:

This materially strengthens Luis Sanchez's authorship beyond later database tradition. It also clarifies the relationship between the two names: Sanchez was operating under the Digitrex name in the authorship record, while Techstar was the claimant/rightsholder.

Important chronology nuance: the copyright registration is dated March 3, 1982, while Digitrex, Inc. was not incorporated in Florida until March 17, 1982. The safest reading is that Sanchez was already using Digitrex as a d/b/a or trade name before incorporation. Do not backdate Digitrex, Inc.'s corporate existence to the copyright date.

How Rock-Ola entered the picture

Cash Box, May 22, 1982, reports a licensing agreement between Rock-Ola Manufacturing Corp. and Techstar, Inc. of Miami, Florida for Techstar's videogame Eyes. The agreement was concluded at a Chicago meeting with Techstar president Bill Olliges. Rock-Ola president Donald C. Rockola said Rock-Ola was pleased to acquire the game “for the coin market.”

The accompanying photograph/caption is unusually valuable because it identifies the principals present:

Controlled rights chain for the U.S. coin-op release: Luis Sanchez / Digitrex development -> Techstar ownership/commercial rights -> Rock-Ola licensed manufacture/marketing for the coin market.

Do not describe Rock-Ola as the original developer of Eyes. Rock-Ola was the licensed coin-op manufacturer/marketer/publisher.

Luis Sanchez and the recorded developer credit

The copyright record directly ties Luis Sanchez d.b.a. Digitrex to the authorship of Eyes. Florida corporate records separately identify Luis Sanchez as the principal officer of Digitrex, Inc. shortly afterward. Keith Smith's interview-based arcade history further identifies Sanchez as a former Allied Leisure / Arcade Engineering engineer who designed Eyes.

Current controlled creator wording: Eyes was principally created/designed by Luis Sanchez, working under the Digitrex name; Techstar held the commercial rights and licensed the game to Rock-Ola.

This is stronger and more precise than the generic “developed by Digitrex Techstar” label used by many modern databases.

Open personnel question: no equally strong evidence has yet identified additional programmers, artists or audio designers for the original game. Do not invent a larger development team.

The former Centuri connection

The contemporary Cash Box photograph identifies Bill Olliges as Techstar and Ed Miller as a Techstar consultant at the Eyes licensing announcement. Smith's Centuri history independently places Olliges and Miller in the former Allied Leisure/Centuri executive group, with Olliges leaving Centuri in 1981 and forming Techstar in Miami. The same history says Miller later formed Telko Properties, which worked with Techstar on game licensing.

This gives Eyes a useful Florida coin-op lineage: it was not an anonymous one-off submitted to Rock-Ola, but a game coming through people with direct Allied/Centuri experience.

The real launch window: May before July

The May 22, 1982 Cash Box licensing article says sample orders would be delivered to Rock-Ola factory distributors beginning in mid-May and that Eyes would be available in both upright and cocktail-table models.

That is primary period evidence of commercial sample distribution in May 1982. It materially predates the widely repeated modern “July 1982” release date.

A later Cash Box manufacturer-equipment compilation lists Rock-Ola Eyes (7/82). This may represent a later full-production/catalog milestone rather than first distribution, but the meaning of the compilation date is not sufficiently explicit to override the contemporaneous mid-May sample-delivery statement.

Chronology control: Do not state simply that Eyes “was released in July 1982.” The primary evidence proves Rock-Ola sample distribution began earlier.

The Eyes trademark stayed with Techstar

A U.S. trademark filing dated June 21, 1982 identifies Techstar, Inc. as owner of the EYES mark for “Video Output Game Machines,” serial no. 73370519. A separate Techstar EYES filing also appears in May 1982 archival trademark indexes.

This independently reinforces the commercial-rights picture from the copyright and Cash Box records: the underlying name/game rights sat with Techstar, while Rock-Ola was the licensed coin-op producer/marketer.

A later rights echo from the home-computer market

A 1983 Roklan advertisement for upcoming Atari-computer software explicitly states that Rockball and Eyes are trademarks of Techstar, Inc. This is later than the arcade launch and should not be used as first-release evidence, but it corroborates continued Techstar ownership/control of the Eyes mark after the Rock-Ola arcade agreement.

A dedicated Rock-Ola production game

The surviving original Rock-Ola Eyes Service Manual and Parts Catalog identifies the upright as Model G-206, Form No. 827 / No. G-6377-1. The parts catalog documents a dedicated Rock-Ola production package including: The manual further specifies a 19-inch vertical raster monitor, with the parts list naming a Wells-Gardner #4951F monitor. The cabinet list carries separate coin-door/price-card provisions for the U.S./Canada, Australia, Belgium, France, Germany, England and Japan, plus 120V and 220/240V electrical arrangements. This is strong evidence that Rock-Ola engineered Eyes as a normal factory product with export-aware cabinet support, even though the underlying game was licensed from Techstar rather than developed by Rock-Ola.

The Rock-Ola license and two cabinet formats

The surviving U.S. Rock-Ola sales flyer states at the bottom: “Manufactured under exclusive license from Digitrex/Techstar.” It identifies the upright as Model G-206 and separately says a cocktail table version, Model GC-206, was available.

Flyer specifications for the upright: The flyer markets the game around eight distinct fields/modes, matching the manual's statement that Eyes consists of eight fields. For this site, this flyer is a high-value publication asset because it simultaneously documents product form, licensing language, model number, cocktail availability, physical specifications and Rock-Ola's own marketing voice.

A Pac-Man-family design, not a simple board swap

Modern MAME places Eyes in `pacman/pacman.cpp`, and current MAME-derived records identify the machine as using a Z80 and Namco-style WSG audio architecture. The Rock-Ola manual, however, proves that the production machine used a dedicated Rock-Ola Eyes PCB assembly, G-6390-A, rather than simply being a ROM swap in a stock Midway/Namco Pac-Man board.

The G-6390-A parts list includes: Controlled wording for the website/master: Eyes uses a Pac-Man-family hardware architecture, but Rock-Ola's production G-206 contained its own documented G-6390-A Eyes board assembly. Do not reduce this to “Eyes was a Pac-Man conversion” unless a specific surviving board is being described.

What operators could set

Rock-Ola's DIP-switch table documents: Normal factory setting is shown as 3 Eyes per player, first bonus at 75,000, 1 coin / 1 play.

The gameplay section also gives field-by-field scoring: enemy-eye values rise from 100 on field 1 to 800 on field 8, while bonus-object values rise from 500 to 4,000. It states a player must average a minimum of 50 points per second to stay alive. This is useful for the later this site gameplay/history section because it is factory documentation rather than retrospective description.

The surviving U.S. program sets

Zaccaria's European version was licensed

Older secondary descriptions have repeatedly called Zaccaria's Eyes a bootleg. The primary surviving Zaccaria flyer disproves that characterization.

The front explicitly says: “Licensed exclusively for Europe” while the back describes Eyes as “constructed under licence from Techstar.” The illustrated title screen reads “ZACCARIA PRESENTS EYES” and the flyer reproduces a 1982 Techstar copyright line.

Independent PCB-preservation work is even stronger. Citylan documents an original Italian Eyes PCB marked: Current MAME has also corrected the classification to Techstar (Zaccaria license).

What the two licenses do—and do not—prove

Rock-Ola's U.S. flyer says the G-206 was manufactured under an exclusive license from Digitrex/Techstar. Zaccaria's own flyer says Zaccaria held the exclusive European license. Therefore the Rock-Ola exclusivity statement cannot safely be interpreted as worldwide exclusivity.

The strongest present interpretation is: The exact written territorial boundary of Rock-Ola's contract remains open because the contract itself has not been found. Website copy should describe the two proven arrangements rather than inventing a worldwide grant.

A different European cabinet, the same game

The Zaccaria flyer depicts a dramatically different dedicated cabinet/art package, while preserving the Techstar game and licensing chain. It shows multiple Zaccaria cabinet formats at the bottom of the flyer (regular, wall cabinet, cocktail table and de luxe cabinet). This makes the European release a useful this site visual case study: same underlying Techstar game, different licensed manufacturer, substantially different cabinet identity.

Eggor: related, but not a regional Eyes set

Current MAME keeps Eggor (Telko, 1983) as a separate machine in the same Pac-Man-family driver rather than as an Eyes clone. Arcade-History describes it as appearing to be based on Eyes and documents the same eight-maze shooter structure with altered characters and scoring.

This is enough to treat Eggor as part of the post-Eyes Techstar/Telko design lineage, but not enough to label it an unauthorized clone. Rights documentation for Eggor itself is still needed.

Gorkans and Mr. TNT: a separate branch

Current MAME-derived records treat Gorkans as a Techstar title in the Pac-Man-family hardware group and Mr. TNT as the closely related Telko version. Preservation discussion and MAME testing show a complicated ROM/encryption relationship, but this is a distinct game family rather than a simple Eyes regional set.

Controlled handling: This matters because this site should show relationships without flattening every code-relative game into “bootleg” or “clone.”

Eyes at Rock-Ola's 1982 product show

Cash Box reported Rock-Ola's Sept. 8-12, 1982 annual distributor meeting/product showing at Kiawah Island under the theme “Looking Good And Playing Great.” The product emphasis was specifically on the firm's Eyes video game and its Deluxe phonograph line. Rock-Ola said the products would soon be available through factory distributors.

This is important context for the earlier May sample-delivery evidence. It suggests Eyes remained a major Rock-Ola sales focus several months later and gives us a stronger commercial story than a single release-date line: mid-May sample distribution, later 1982 production/marketing, and a September distributor presentation centered on Eyes.

## Controlled conclusion from Eyes Part 2 Eyes was a fully engineered Rock-Ola product despite being externally created. Rock-Ola manufactured the U.S. G-206 around a dedicated documented cabinet and G-6390-A PCB, offered a GC-206 cocktail version, and supported domestic/export electrical and coinage configurations. Its hardware belongs to the Pac-Man architectural family, but the factory machine should not be described as merely a Pac-Man ROM conversion.

The international rights picture is also now substantially clearer. Rock-Ola's own flyer states an exclusive Digitrex/Techstar license, while Zaccaria's original 1982 material and surviving PCB markings prove that Zaccaria held a legitimate Techstar license for Europe. The old “Zaccaria bootleg” story is therefore wrong and should be actively corrected in the account.

For this site, Eyes now supports a particularly strong evidence-first mini-narrative: Luis Sanchez / Digitrex created it -> Techstar controlled the commercial rights -> Rock-Ola manufactured and marketed the U.S. G-206/GC-206 -> Zaccaria separately manufactured the licensed European version in its own cabinet family.

## research set 07 — Eyes special-priority deep dive, Part 3: reception, performance, preservation and legacy

Route testing, earnings, and what the claim means

Cash Box, July 17, 1982, reported a direct statement from Rock-Ola vice president of sales Ron Moskal that the new Eyes video game had maintained “cash earnings within the top five pieces” during eight to ten weeks of testing at arcades and locations across the United States. Moskal added that Rock-Ola distributors regarded it as a “hot piece, with enormous potential.” The item again identifies Eyes as licensed by Rock-Ola from Techstar, Inc. of Miami.

This is the strongest period performance evidence recovered so far. It is a manufacturer/sales-executive claim rather than an independently audited national earnings table, so website copy should attribute it to Rock-Ola rather than convert it into an objective nationwide ranking. Even with that qualification, it proves Rock-Ola was publicly reporting strong route-test performance by mid-July.

A national advertising push

A full-page Rock-Ola advertisement in Cash Box, July 3, 1982, uses the large headline “LOOKING GOOD” with eyeglasses incorporated into the lettering and promotes EYES as Rock-Ola's newest video game, ending with the line “plays for keeps.”

This gives this site a strong period visual and confirms that Eyes was not a quiet one-off license: Rock-Ola was buying prominent trade-ad space for it during the commercial ramp-up.

A game to watch in 1982

The first issue of Video Games Player (Fall 1982), in its “Arcade Parade” column, included Eyes (Rock-Ola) in its short list of “New games to watch for.” This is not a formal review or earnings ranking, but it is useful independent consumer-facing evidence that the title had enough visibility to be singled out among the season's incoming arcade releases.

By Nov. 13, 1982, Cash Box reported that Rock-Ola VP Ron Moskal was pleased Eyes had been doing well both in player popularity and in reliability. Again, this is a Rock-Ola executive's statement and should be attributed, but it demonstrates that the company was still presenting Eyes as a successful and dependable product late in the year.

Together with the already preserved Sept. 8-12 distributor-meeting emphasis, the period arc is now clear: sample distribution in May -> prominent July advertising and claimed strong route tests -> fall consumer visibility -> September distributor-centerpiece status -> favorable Rock-Ola popularity/reliability comments in November.

Why “July 1982” is an incomplete release date

Cash Box equipment listings use 7/82 for Eyes, which likely explains the July date repeated by later databases. But primary reporting already proves that sample orders were scheduled for Rock-Ola factory distributors beginning in mid-May 1982, and the July 17 item describes eight to ten weeks of testing at U.S. arcades/locations. Therefore July cannot safely be used as the first time commercial machines reached the field.

Controlled chronology wording for this site: Use “commercial rollout began by mid-May 1982; July appears to mark a later full-production/catalog phase” unless a better primary source explicitly defines the July milestone.

The unrecovered RePlay chart claim

Keith Smith's Golden Age Arcade Historian Rock-Ola history states that Eyes reached #5 on RePlay's software charts. Smith's work is detailed and heavily sourced, and the claim is consistent with the strong Cash Box route-test report, but the original RePlay chart has not yet been independently recovered in this project.

Control: retain as a corroborative secondary lead, not as a locked primary-statistic claim in final website prose unless the period RePlay chart is obtained.

Why serious players learned Eyes fast

Rock-Ola's G-206 manual says the player must score a minimum of 50 points per second to stay alive, recommends following a zig-zag route while shooting bonus objects quickly, and caps bonus lives at four per game. These rules materially shape expert play: the game actively discourages leisurely point pressing and turns later play into rapid execution plus recovery from deviations.

Modern specialist play discussion independently reflects those factory rules. Experienced players advise clearing boards rapidly rather than point pressing and developing repeatable routes, especially around difficult target positions. Importantly, high-level players also describe the patterns as fragile: small timing changes can alter enemy behavior, so improvisation when a route breaks remains a major skill.

This modern community material is legacy/strategy evidence, not period reception. It is suitable for a later-reputation sidebar, especially because it connects cleanly to the original manual's scoring design.

High scores and competitive play

Electronic Games published a national high-score listing in 1983 that included Eyes (Rock-Ola) with Craig Seitz of Cuba, Missouri — 4,663,820 points. This establishes score-chasing around Eyes within roughly a year of release, independent of later collector nostalgia.

A much later authenticated event result demonstrates the title's continuing competitive life: Aurcade records James Dinndorf — 26,153,650 on Eyes during Grinkfest 2016, held Oct. 14-15, 2016.

Do not present modern scores as directly comparable with the 1983 magazine score without controlling board/set/settings and verification rules. Their value here is historical continuity: Eyes generated organized score interest both near its original era and decades later.

What surviving machines can—and cannot—tell us

The current VAPS / International Arcade Museum census lists 45 member-owned Eyes game entries (13 explicitly upright/standard and 32 cabinet type unspecified) and 27 board entries. These are collector-submitted records, may not represent unique surviving units in every case, and are not statistically suitable for estimating Rock-Ola production volume.

Multiple detailed restoration threads nevertheless demonstrate surviving dedicated Rock-Ola cabinets, original marquees/bezels/control panels, and recurring deterioration of Rock-Ola's fiberboard/MDF cabinet construction. One 2019 machine was recovered from the garage of a former family-fun-center operator with its internals still functioning. Another 2016 restoration documents an Eyes installation in a blue Jump Bug cabinet and argues it was probably a Rock-Ola factory conversion; that factory-conversion interpretation remains collector inference, not proven factory documentation.

this site should use survivor material to illustrate preservation and cabinet construction, while keeping “factory conversion” language explicitly provisional unless Rock-Ola paperwork is found.

The Atari 8-bit version that never reached stores

Roklan advertised an Atari-computer version of Eyes in 1983. A Roklan ad in BYTE (June 1983) lists Eyes among titles “COMING SOON” and explicitly says “Rockball and Eyes are trademarks of Techstar, Inc.” This is excellent post-arcade rights evidence because it shows Techstar still controlling the Eyes mark while licensing/planning consumer software outside the Rock-Ola coin-op channel.

Modern Atari preservation sources classify the research as unreleased/prototype. Atari Compendium lists Eyes (Roklan 1983 unreleased), while Atarimania catalogs a Roklan Atari 8-bit Eyes prototype (year 1984 in its database), marks the original dump as missing, and preserves advertising scans.

Taken together, the evidence shows: an Atari 8-bit home port was definitely announced and marketed by Roklan in 1983 and is preserved as a prototype/unreleased project; no completed commercial home release has yet been proven. Therefore blanket claims that “no home version of Eyes was made” are too strong. A better this site sidebar is “The home port that almost happened.”

The Game That Explains The Company

There are still things we do not know about Eyes. No reliable production total has been found. The company that physically fabricated the Techstar-marked Japanese boards remains unidentified. The exact contract between Sanchez, Digitrex, and Techstar has not survived in the accessible record.

Eyes provides an unusually complete record for a Techstar title: a named creator, Techstar’s documented rights position, a contemporary licensing agreement, two manufacturing partners, multiple cabinet formats, service documentation, advertisements, surviving program variants and physical boards. Together they show how Techstar could be central to a game without writing every line of code or building every cabinet. It could acquire or control rights, arrange manufacturing, license a title into different territories, supply hardware, and connect independent creators with companies able to put machines onto arcade floors.

That arrangement carried a Florida-created game into Rock-Ola cabinets in America and Zaccaria cabinets in Europe. Macho Mouse involved a different combination of companies, with Amenip Corporation supplying the documented work.
